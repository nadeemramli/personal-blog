# Capabilities and Competency — Series Outline

The aim before the shot. Income 101 told you why income matters and how to position yourself. Multiplying Money 101 told you what to do with money once you have it. This series sits in between those two and answers the harder question: *what specifically should you get good at, and why does anyone pay for it?*

The whole series runs on one move repeated at different zoom levels: read a business well enough to see where money is actually made and lost, then locate the single skill that fixes the weakest spot, then find the person inside that business who owns the number you can move. That person is your buyer.

## What this series is for

Three series form a chain in the Rich section:

1. [[Part 1.0 - What Income Actually Is|Income 101]] (why build a capability, and how to package yourself)
2. **Capabilities and Competency** (which capability is worth building, for whom)
3. [[Part 1.0 - Allocation The Sankey Mindset|Multiplying Money 101]] (what to do with the money the capability earns)

A fourth piece (the actual learning-and-practice mechanics) and the sales-and-marketing mechanism both live downstream of this series. This is the targeting layer. Once you finish it, you walk away with a named, specific, phase-appropriate skill and a named buyer.

## The spine in one paragraph

All businesses want profit. Profit splits two ways: grow what comes in, or shrink what goes out. Every learnable skill plugs into one of those branches. But at any moment, a business only has one main thing holding it back (the bottleneck), and value flows to whoever fixes that one thing. The bottleneck moves as the business ages: early-stage companies are stuck on customers, growing ones on repeatable sales, scaling ones on operations and money, mature ones on cost and defence. So the same skill is worth wildly different amounts depending on the stage of the business you point it at. That single idea is what this series teaches you to use.

## The full module map

### Part 1 — How to think before you start

**Part 1.0 — Why Understand Business Before Picking a Skill**

You cannot choose what to get good at until you can see where a business makes and loses money. Without that, "skill" is just a list of certificates. The warning lands here too: before you have worked inside a real business, frameworks are just more noise. After some real exposure, those same frameworks cut the noise. So every lesson in this series gets pointed at something you have actually touched (an internship, a side hustle, the shop down the street), not a hypothetical company.

*Connects to:* sets the operating rule for the whole series (learn the lens, then point it at something real). Hands the reader the recurring closing move: take the framework, run it on a real business you know, write down what it shows and what it misses.

### Part 2 — How a business actually works (the engine)

**Part 2.0 — The Profit Engine**

Profit equals money in minus money out. Every useful skill either brings money in or cuts money out. Then the load-bearing idea: at any one time, a business has one main thing holding it back. Fixing that one thing is worth a lot. Polishing anything else is worth almost nothing. This is the bottleneck idea (theory of constraints, in plain words), and it is the single concept that lets the rest of the series do real work.

*Connects to:* this is the engine everything else explains. Next you check whether the engine is even sound.

**Part 2.1 — The Numbers That Tell You If It Works (Unit Economics)**

A few simple numbers: what it costs to acquire one customer, how much that customer is worth across the time they stay, and the profit left on each sale after the costs of delivering it. If those numbers don't add up, no amount of skill saves the business (and that, by itself, is a signal worth reading: walk away). Light treatment of CAC, LTV, payback, contribution margin, fixed vs variable cost (just the vocabulary).

*Connects to:* once you know the engine and you know whether it works, you can read the structure.

**Part 2.2 — The Map of a Business (Zoom Out, Zoom In)**

Zoom out (Cedric Chen's triad): money (capital), running it (operations), customers (market). Every business is juggling these three. Zoom in (the Business Model Canvas): the same thing at higher resolution (segments, value prop, channels, relationships, revenue, activities, resources, partners, cost structure). The frame: BMC is the zoom-in of the triad, not a separate framework. Show how the nine BMC blocks nest under the three triad nodes.

*Connects to:* now that you can see the parts, you name the jobs those parts are doing.

**Part 2.3 — The Four Jobs Every Business Has**

Every business is trying to do four things: grow the flow, keep what flows in, defend it from competitors, and multiply it through a team. Each job has parts that do it (grow = sales/marketing + ops; keep = finance/accounting; defend = strategy/power, covered in 3.1; multiply = management/people). This is the fix for "it's all just marketing." Every function matters and each one is a real place a skill can live.

*Connects to:* now you ask where value actually sits inside those jobs, and why the same skill is worth different amounts in different businesses.

### Part 3 — Where value lives, and why the same skill is worth different amounts

**Part 3.0 — Making Value vs. Keeping Value**

Making value means building something people want (product, R&D, a rare specialty, great quality, real speed). Keeping value means making sure the profit stays with you (good pricing, tight costs, smart capital). The industry decides which of these is the centre of gravity. In software, the tech makes value (it *is* the product). In manufacturing, operations make value (the factory *is* the product). In ecommerce, the product is the product and tech/ops mostly keep value (unless delivery itself becomes the reason people buy, in which case it flips back to making value). One rule of thumb: the part of the business that is the reason customers pick you is "making value." The same skill in a different business, where it isn't the reason people pick, is just plumbing. Before deciding a skill is worth getting good at, ask: in this industry, is this skill the product, or the plumbing?

*Connects to:* but making and keeping value isn't enough on its own. Competitors copy you. That's Power.

**Part 3.1 — Power: Why Some Businesses Keep Their Profit**

Power is anything that lets a business hold onto its profit when competitors try to take it. The seven powers in plain language: scale (bigger and cheaper), network (more users make it more valuable), switching costs (customers can't easily leave), brand (people pay more for trust), cornered resource (only you have access), counter-positioning (an incumbent can't copy you without hurting themselves), process power (something only you have learned to do, over years). The selling point (USP) and steady demand at good prices are downstream of power. Without power, whatever value you create gets competed away.

*Connects to:* power and value depend on when. Which is stage.

**Part 3.2 — The Stages of a Business**

Phase × constraint, the centrepiece teaching tool. Early-stage: the question is "does anyone want this and will they pay?" — bottleneck is the market. Gold skills: customer discovery, scrappy selling, building a first version. Growing: "can we get customers steadily and profitably?" — bottleneck is repeatable sales. Gold skills: performance marketing, sales process, funnel analytics. Scaling: "can we deliver at volume without breaking?" — bottleneck moves to operations and capital. Gold skills: systems design, hiring, finance. Mature: "can we stay efficient and defend our spot?" — bottleneck is cost and retention. The takeaway: the same skill is worth wildly different amounts depending on the stage. Timing is part of value.

*Connects to:* now you go deep on the two biggest levers a skill can pull.

### Part 4 — The two big levers, up close

**Part 4.0 — Bringing in Sales**

The sales-and-marketing mechanism. How strangers become leads, leads become buyers, and buyers come back. The funnel material from earlier work lands here. Why this lever leads: nothing happens in a business until something is sold. It is the first thing that must work. But (per the bottleneck idea) once a business can sell, sales stops being the constraint and value moves to the next weak spot. Sales is always necessary, not always the bottleneck.

*Connects to:* the other end of the engine is spending less.

**Part 4.1 — Cutting Cost Without Breaking Things**

The cost side: operations, supply chain, customer service. Doing the same work cheaper, faster, with less waste. Keeping the customers you already have, so you don't keep paying to replace them. A ringgit saved is a ringgit of profit, same as a ringgit earned. The Malaysian context here is honest: in a market with smaller revenue ceilings than the US, the cost lever often pays out faster than the revenue lever.

*Connects to:* both levers only pay off if you know who inside the company owns them. Which is the next module.

### Part 5 — How work gets organized, and who actually pays you

**Part 5.0 — Goals and Structure (How Companies Decide Who Does What)**

How companies cascade a big goal into smaller goals across teams (OKR / KPI vocabulary, kept light), and who owns which number. Why this matters to you: you sell your skill to the person who owns the number you can move. The founder owns profit. The CMO owns revenue. The COO owns cost-to-serve. The CFO owns capture and capital. Find the owner of the weak number and you have found your buyer.

*Connects to:* now you put the whole chain together.

### Part 6 — Putting it together

**Part 6.0 — From a Business to the Exact Skill**

The full move, start to finish, run live on a real business: read its stage → find the bottleneck → locate the weak number in the engine → name the job that needs doing → name the skill that does it → name the person who'll pay for it. Walk it through on a concrete Malaysian example so the chain *feels* real instead of theoretical.

*Connects to:* the last step is to turn the lens on the reader.

**Part 6.1 — Run It on Yourself**

You are a one-person business. You make value (your skill, your output). You keep value (your pricing and positioning, whether you get paid what you're worth or competed to commodity rates). You need power (your USP, your rarity, your reputation, the switching costs you build with clients). The same four questions you ask of a company, you ask of yourself. Hand-off: now go build it (the learning-and-practice series) and now go sell it to the person who owns the metric (the sales mechanism module 4.0 already opens).

## The voice spine (so every article sounds like the rest)

(Pulled from the running house-style and the conversation that generated this outline.)

Plain words. "Plumbing" instead of "ancillary infrastructure." "The number" instead of "the KPI." "What's holding it back" before introducing "bottleneck." Bombastic words like "orthogonal" or "fractal" are banned, even when they're technically the right word; if a concept needs that word, it needs a better explanation instead.

Every article opens with a real situation (terrain first), introduces the framework as a lens, forces the reader to apply it to a business they've actually touched, and closes with reflection on what the lens revealed *and* what it missed. That last step honours the pain × reflection model and keeps the framework subordinate to experience. Don't skip it.

Value creation vs. value capture gets taught early and gets quietly reused everywhere. The reason: a learner can pour years into a "valuable" skill that no business has figured out how to capture profit from, and end up unpaid. This is the hidden link to the sales series.

Reader-voice rules: "you" is the reader, never the author. Headers are declarative, not self-praising. Em-dashes stay out of prose (use parentheses for asides, colons or sentence-splits otherwise). Em-dashes are fine in titles, group labels, citations.
