## Running a course is reading its numbers

[[Part 1.2 - Course Design|Course Design]] handed you the course: its direction, its length, its food, its KPIs and its place in the sequence. Part 2 will hand you the compounds and Part 3 the defences. This page is what you do with all of it between the first injection and the last: the order in which you reach for more when progress stalls, the way a compound is added, the clocks the course runs on, the one decision a stalled lift actually asks, and the money, counted the way the Start Here essays say every bill must be counted.

It is also what makes a course a course rather than a cycle ([[Part 1.2 - Course Design|1.2]]): a cycle repeats a recipe; a course reads its own numbers and changes. A protocol is a fixed recipe, and it goes stale the moment your body responds to it. ==A control loop is alive: it reads a metric, compares it to a target, and adjusts.== That difference is the entire philosophy of the series ([[Part 1.0 - The Decision|lowest effective dose]], [[Part 2.0 - The Anabolic Pathways|every pathway]], measured relentlessly, on a calendar) expressed as machinery instead of prose.[^1]

## Four clocks, one veto

A course is not one decision made over and over. Four things are being run at once, on different clocks and against different gauges, and most bad decisions come from not seeing which one a problem lives in. The endocrine signal is adjusted on the eight-week panel; the food lever moves week to week off the logbook and the mirror; the accelerators switch on and off with the phase; the shield never switches off at all. Trying to run all of that as a single decision is how people end up recklessly escalating one number (usually testosterone) because they cannot see where the problem actually is. Separate the four and every problem has an address.

| What you run | The lever | The gauge | The clock, or the trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The signal** (the base engine) | The testosterone base, the DHT-family dial, HCG through every cruise; go lateral, not vertical; one compound at a time | Hematocrit (under ~52%) read with a sensitive estradiol | The ~week-8 panel |
| **The food** (the fuel lever) | Calories, digestion, progressive overload, insulin sensitivity; the ladder below; the course calendar | The gym logbook and body-fat percentage | Stalled strength, or losing the abs |
| **The accelerators** (the deployment) | The phase's short-term tools, tiered to the job | The cut's tier, set by body fat | Entering a specialised phase |
| **The shield** (the longevity layer) | The cardiovascular and renal protective core | Blood pressure (under ~120/80) and the lipid panel | Every morning, on a schedule |

*If you internalise nothing else on this page, internalise these four rows; every detail in Parts 1 to 3 hangs off one of them.*

**The signal: go lateral, not vertical.** You hold testosterone at a known, tolerated baseline and resist the urge to keep climbing it. Forcing it higher drags estrogen up with it and pushes you toward harsh [[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|aromatase inhibitors]] that wreck your lipids, a tax on the shield. ==Instead of escalating vertically, you add laterally:== a compound on a different pathway (the DHT-family dial, the minimum 19-nor, the peptides) raises output on a lever the base is not already pulling, while the stack largely self-regulates its estradiol. Output rises without harshness rising; why wet versus dry is a tunable setting rather than a property is in [[Part 2.1 - The Anabolic Steroid Family Tree#The family tree in motion: wet vs. dry is a setting, not a property|the Family Tree]].[^2] This is the most strictly data-driven of the four: ==(is progress still coming? → dose-up or food-up) + (are the compounds tolerated?) = what the blood says.== You do not adjust the signal on feel; you adjust it on the [[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring|mid-course panel]]. If the markers are clean and progress is live, the signal stays where it is. If they are skewed, the [[Part 3.4 - When the Numbers Move|number is the boss]] and you pull back regardless of the mirror.

**The food decides the signal's next move.** "Progress stalled" does not mean "raise the dose." The food lever gets first refusal (add food, fix training), and only when extra food is turning to fat instead of strength does the question pass to the signal. ==Food is the cheaper lever; pull it before the drug lever every time.== Get this wrong and you either leave free growth on the table (under-eating a working course) or pour fat onto an engine that cannot partition it (over-eating past the body-fat gate). The gates the food lever reads (never a surplus above ~15%; push at 8 to 12%; pull back at 15 to 17%), the rates and the eight-week audit are in [[Part 1.2 - Course Design|Course Design]].[^3]

**The food also sets the accelerators' intensity.** Your body-fat tier *is* your deployment tier. A cut that starts from "a bit soft" gets the light tier (more steps, a modest deficit, a gentle thyroid and glucose nudge); a genuine mid-cut gets the medium tier (the mitochondrial and exercise-mimetic stack, the gentler β3 route); only a lean physique chasing a final peak earns the heavy tier. ==You escalate the deployment tier to match the body-fat tier, never the reverse,== and the moment a marker the [[Part 3.2 - Continuous Monitoring|monitoring]] watches goes the wrong way, the tier steps down. The accelerators do not build the base; they sharpen or unstick a phase, the [[Part 2.2 - The Extra Compounds|margin on the margin]], and they come on hard, do the job, and come straight back off (a mini-cut is aggressive, brief, usually two to four weeks, and hyper-efficient at restoring insulin sensitivity without crashing training). The three-tier menu is in [[Part 2.2 - The Extra Compounds|The Extra Compounds]].[^4]

**The shield caps everything.** It holds the veto. If hematocrit, blood pressure or lipids break their limits, the signal comes down and the accelerators come off, no matter what the mirror or the logbook say. The shield is a small, permanent core of multi-job ancillaries anchored to the morning routine: a statin and, where needed, ezetimibe for the lipid hit; an [[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|ARB]] and low-dose tadalafil for pressure and the endothelium; a GLP-class agent and L-carnitine for the metabolic side; baby aspirin for the clotting risk the others do not cover. It is dosed on a schedule and never in response to a problem, because by the time the problem shows up the damage is partly done. Remember the asymmetry from [[Part 3.4 - When the Numbers Move|Part 3.4]]: the liver regenerates, the kidneys and vessels largely do not. You protect by system, proactively, because the risks of a stack multiply rather than add. The agents, their within-family selection and their cadence are in [[Part 3.3 - Protection|Protection]] and [[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|Choosing Your Ancillaries]].[^5]

**And the calendar bounds the signal, not just the panel.** The base engine does not run hot forever; the course's end date, the [[Part 1.2 - Course Design|on/down-period rhythm]] and the [[Part 3.7 - Coming Off and PCT|coming-off sequence]] are the longer clock underneath the eight-week one.

> [!tip] The whole machine in one sentence
> ==Run the smallest signal that is still progressing, fuelled by the food lever first and the drug lever last, accelerated only as hard as the phase demands, underneath a shield that never switches off and always has the final veto.==

## The adjustment ladder: food first, training second, dose last

The single most important discipline in running a course is not *what* you run; it is the **order in which you reach for more.** When progress stalls, drugs are the *last* rung, not the first:[^6]

1. **Add food first.** Bump calories ~10% and give it a few weeks.
2. **Adjust training**: volume, intensity, recovery.
3. **Only then increase the dose**, and only when the extra food is producing *fat* instead of strength or size.

==This is what "milk the dose" means: squeeze the living soul out of your current dose before adding to it.== You stay at a base for as long as you are still getting stronger on it. The course is a tool to overcome a *biological* limit you have genuinely hit, not a shortcut around food and training you have not optimised yet.

> [!tip] Why you'd ever go beyond a replacement base
> Two legitimate reasons, both downstream of the ladder. **Maintenance cost:** as you carry more muscle, more of your protein synthesis is spent just *holding* it, leaving less surplus for new growth, so the hormonal signal eventually has to rise. **A true plateau:** once natural progress has completely flatlined, raising the androgen signal elicits the next jump. Neither is a reason to *start* high; they are reasons to *climb*, slowly.

The top of that climb is what the sources call **"calculated unhealthiness"**: the final push of a course where blood markers skew while you chase a peak. It is *calculated* because it is deliberate, monitored and time-boxed, and because the instant lipids or blood pressure go *severely* off, the [[Part 1.0 - The Decision|lowest effective dose]] wins. Calculated unhealthiness is the cue to end the course and [[Part 1.2 - Course Design|cruise]], not to push harder.

## Adding a compound: the sequencing rules

When the ladder finally says "add a drug," *how* you add it matters as much as *what*:[^6]

- **One compound at a time, with bloods 4 weeks in.** Add a single variable, then read how your lipids, liver, estrogen and hematocrit responded *to that variable* before stacking the next. You can't troubleshoot a five-compound soup.
- **Exhaust the bioidenticals before the exotics.** Maximise testosterone, then GH, then (for the full-time athlete only) insulin, the three hormones the body already makes and regulates, *before* reaching for harsh compounds. GH and insulin build a phenomenal physique at lower health cost than piling on androgens; the first transition into an enhanced state is sustainable precisely because it minimises the exotic metabolic load on the liver and kidneys. It is the [[Part 2.1 - The Anabolic Steroid Family Tree|learning ladder]] (Test → DHT family → 19-nor) expressed as a dosing philosophy.
- **The 19-nor is near-last, not a beginner tool.** Exhaust the DHT family (Primobolan, Masteron) first, and reserve trenbolone for a specific goal at an advanced level, at the minimum that does the job; its [[Part 2.2 - The Extra Compounds|neuro and prolactin]] cost is real.
- **Mind the hematocrit ceiling.** Every erythropoietic compound you stack on the testosterone base (the 19-nor, the wet oral) thickens the blood on top of what the base already does. ==If hematocrit climbs sky-high despite hydration and blood donation, the erythropoietic add-ons come *off the table* first, tren included==; no compound is worth stroke-territory blood viscosity ([[Part 3.4 - When the Numbers Move|Part 3.4]]). (Older stacks built on boldenone and nandrolone were notorious for this; neither covers a node in this series' designs, see [[Part 2.0 - The Anabolic Pathways|Part 2.0]].)
- **Don't mistake an SHBG agent for an AI.** Masteron's job in the build (and Proviron's, in older stacks) is raising *free* testosterone by binding SHBG ([[Part 2.0 - The Anabolic Pathways|Pathway 2]]) and competing at the estrogen receptor; it is **not** a reliable estrogen blocker. A high aromatiser still needs the DHT dial, and failing that a proper AI or SERM ([[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|Part 3.5]]). SHBG itself is a *vital carrier*, not an enemy: it delivers androgens and estrogens to tissue via the SHBG-receptor complex, and crushing it through oral abuse diminishes libido and anabolism.[^7]

## The blood-work cadence

Blood work is the one thread that never breaks across the courses: it is the gauge for the signal and the proof that the shield is holding. The cadence tightens when something changes and relaxes when things are stable, but it never stops. What each panel contains is [[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring|Part 3.1]]'s; this is the clock it runs on:

| When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Baseline** (before anything) | The full panel ([[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring|Part 3.1]]) | The reference every later number is read against |
| **~4 weeks** into any new compound | Early lipids, hematocrit, liver, glucose | Catch a bad responder before it compounds |
| **~8 weeks** (mid-course) | The core decision panel: CBC (hematocrit), lipids, sensitive E2; prolactin on a 19-nor, liver on an oral, glucose and HbA1c on GH ([[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring#On-course monitoring: what each checkpoint pulls|what each checkpoint pulls]]) | The week-8 trigger: adjust or hold the signal |
| **Monthly** while on orals or GH | Liver enzymes and fasting glucose | The two markers orals and GH move fastest |
| **Quarterly**, year-round | The full panel | The non-negotiable floor, on *or* off |
| **Before any compound change** | A targeted panel | Never add a variable blind |
| **End of course**, before PCT | The mid-course set plus LH, FSH, total and free testosterone | How suppressed you are; the baseline for recovery |
| **After PCT** (4 to 8 weeks), or at the end of a cruise | The recovery panel (testosterone, LH, FSH, E2, SHBG) | Confirm the axis actually [[Part 3.7 - Coming Off and PCT|came back]] before the next push |
| **Ad hoc** | Whatever the symptom points at | A symptom is a draw, not a guess |
| **Every 1 to 2 years**, once you have been enhanced for years | Echocardiogram and ECG | Cardiac remodelling is silent until it isn't ([[Part 3.3 - Protection|Part 3.3]]) |
| **Every 3 years** (yearly with a family history), whenever GH or IGF-1 is in the plan | The cancer markers ([[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring|Part 3.1]]) | Growth signals grow whatever is already there |

> [!important] Minimum quarterly, monthly for the fast movers
> If budget forces a choice, the liver and glucose markers are the ones to run monthly while on a course (orals and GH move them quickest), with the full quarterly panel as the floor. The [[Part 3.2 - Continuous Monitoring|home devices]] (a BP cuff, a glucometer, a wearable) fill every gap between draws, so you are never flying blind.

## Reading the panel: a single decision

Make it concrete. Say it is week 9 of a build course, and your bench has stalled for two weeks. The untrained instinct is "add testosterone." The course asks four questions instead:

1. **Food first.** Are you still lean (abs visible, under ~15%)? If yes, this is a *food* problem: add carbs, not drugs. Stall solved, no change to the signal.
2. **If you are already eating big and getting soft,** the food lever says hold or cut, which may mean a short mini-cut (an accelerator deployed at the tier your body fat sets), not more food and not more gear.
3. **Only if food and training are genuinely maxed and you are lean** does the question reach the signal, and even then you reach for a *lateral* addition before climbing testosterone, one compound at a time, and only if the week-8 panel was clean.
4. **Through all of it, the shield is watching.** If blood pressure or hematocrit is already near the ceiling, the answer to "can I add anything?" is simply *no*, and the course is winding down.

One stalled lift, four clocks, and the *address of the problem* falls out of the panel. That is the entire value of running it as a system, and it is the reasoning [[Part 1.2 - Course Design#The courses in sequence: the first course to the fifth year|the sequence of courses]] applies, course after course.

## The bill

Most people think the hard part of enhanced bodybuilding is affording it. It is not: ==the hard part is the *uncertainty*, and the fix is two documents, a **budgeting plan** and a **stock plan**.== Once you know exactly what the course and the year look like, the spending becomes predictable, and a surprising truth falls out of the maths: **the drugs and the ancillaries are cheaper than almost everyone assumes.** Bought sensibly, in bulk, a protective stack costs less per month than a couple of restaurant dinners. The real, recurring money is in **food and daily consumables**; the [[Part 4.0 - Pharmacology|90%]] of the 90/10 rule is also 90% of the budget.[^8]

The bill is counted here, at the end of Part 1 and before a single compound is chosen, because [[The Upward Spiral — How the Order Compounds|Start Here]] says every bill is counted before the bet is placed. It lives on the running page because it is a running number: the stock depletes on the same calendar the panel is drawn on. Four steps: plan the course, then list every variable; predict the stock, then the cost; hold a worked stock plan; then budget the part that actually moves the needle.

### Step 1: plan the course, then list every variable

You cannot budget what you have not planned. Start from the course and [[Part 1.2 - Course Design#The courses in sequence: the first course to the fifth year|its place in the sequence]], then write down **every variable that will draw money** over that window, not just the obvious gear. There are three layers to buy, and the order you buy them in matters:

- **The compounds**: each one's dose × weeks is the total needed (Step 2 turns it into boxes and vials). For the sequence in Course Design: a testosterone base, the [[Part 2.1 - The Anabolic Steroid Family Tree|DHT-family]] dial (Primobolan-class) for the build, [[Part 3.7 - Coming Off and PCT|HCG and HMG]] for the down-periods, GH with T4 and the GLP-class agent for the build blocks, and the phase's oral (wet for the build, dry for the cut), only as the sequencing rules above allow.
- **The ancillary tiers** ([[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|3.5]]): the **all-year baby-dose core** (ARB, tadalafil, aspirin, rosuvastatin with or without ezetimibe) that runs continuously, *plus* the **situational tools** (TUDCA and NAC during orals, P5P with a 19-nor, T4 with GH, a glucose agent with GH).
- **Bloodwork** ([[Part 3.1 - Medical Screening and Monitoring|3.1]], on the cadence above): a real, recurring line, the comprehensive baseline panel, then quarterly panels, with extra draws around compound changes.
- **Monitoring gear** ([[Part 3.2 - Continuous Monitoring|3.2]]), mostly one-off: a BP cuff, a glucometer, a wearable.[^9]
- **Daily consumables**: the fast movers from [[Part 3.6 - Electrolyte Management|electrolytes]], creatine, omega-3, and the liver and antioxidant supports.

> [!tip] Buy the shield before the gear
> The single most common mistake is buying a full course and a token packet of ancillaries. Invert it: the shield is the thing that runs 365 days, so it is the thing you stock *first* and deepest. The gear is seasonal; the shield is permanent.

> [!tip] Name the variables that *change* the bill mid-course
> A budget breaks when an unplanned variable appears. The predictable ones, with examples: **a dose escalation** (more testosterone is more vials); **adding a compound** (a second injectable or an oral mid-year is a new line, and perhaps more frequent bloods); **a cut course** ([[Part 2.2 - The Extra Compounds|the cut's tools]], more electrolytes); **the hot season** ([[Part 3.6 - Electrolyte Management|more water, salt and potassium]]); and **more bloodwork** whenever you change anything. Write these as "if X, then plus RM Y" so a mid-year change is a known cost, not a panic.

### Step 2: predict the stock, then the cost

With the plan written, the arithmetic is simple, and it exposes the bulk-buying reality that makes this affordable.

**Convert the plan to quantity.** Dose × duration is the total; divide by package size. A daily ancillary at one tablet a day for a 120-day phase is 120 tablets, which is however many boxes that is. A weekly compound over twenty weeks is a known number of vials.

**The bulk-box reality.** In practice, tablets and supplements are sold in **multi-box quantities**, so the *upfront* cart looks expensive while each item lasts months. The number that matters is not the cart total; it is the **true monthly burn rate**:

> [!important] True monthly burn rate
> ==**Monthly cost = (total cart cost ÷ total supply-days) × 30.**== A box that costs RM100 and lasts 600 days is RM5 a month, not RM100. Most of the protective core is in this category: cheap *per month* despite a chunky one-time outlay. Always budget on the burn rate, not the receipt.

**Tablet-splitting stretches supply.** Many ancillaries are run at a fraction of a tablet. Split the higher-strength statin tablet and the supply lasts twice as long; halve the sleep-aid tablet and a two-box order doubles in lifespan. ==Buying the higher-strength tablet and splitting it is often the single biggest saver,== because it both lowers the dose (the [[Part 1.0 - The Decision|lowest effective]] rule) and stretches the box.

### Step 3: a worked stock plan to year-end

Here is the method as a concrete, illustrative plan: a protective core plus a moderate course, costed on burn rate and projected to "covers me to year-end." *Figures are representative Malaysian-ringgit reference points, not a price list; the working doses are operating-layer numbers, so the table shows each item's cadence instead. Round to your own sources and labs.*

> [!example]- The worked stock plan
> | Item | Cadence | Bought | Supply lifespan | Monthly (burn rate) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **An ARB** | daily | bulk boxes | ~4 months | ~RM35 |
> | **A statin** | daily, tablet split | bulk boxes | ~1.5 years | ~RM6 to 12 |
> | **Aspirin** | daily | bulk boxes | ~1.5 years | ~RM5 |
> | **Tadalafil** | daily | bulk | ~6 months | ~RM15 to 25 |
> | **TUDCA and NAC** | every other day, oral phases only | per oral run | matches the oral run | situational |
> | **A sleep aid** | nightly, tablet split | 2 boxes | ~40 to 80 days | ~RM37 to 73 |
> | **The testosterone base** | weekly | per course | one course | per course |
> | **Bloodwork** | baseline plus quarterly | per draw | per draw | ~RM50 to 130 a month, amortised |
>
> The *protective core* (ARB, statin, aspirin, tadalafil) comes to roughly **RM60 to 80 a month** on burn rate, even though the upfront cart, buying every box at once, might be **RM500 to 600**. Split the splittable tablets and it drops further. The point of the table is not the exact ringgit; it is that ==once the bulk boxes are bought, the *protective* monthly cost is small and you are stocked for months.== You prep once, then mostly re-buy the fast movers.

This is how you prep to year-end: buy the long-life items in bulk now, mark each item's run-out date on a calendar, and only ever re-order the things that genuinely deplete monthly.

### Step 4: budget the part that actually costs more

Here is the reveal the maths forces. Once the pharma is bought in bulk, ==the **recurring** budget is dominated by the boring daily things, exactly the [[Part 4.0 - Pharmacology|90%]] that builds the physique in the first place:==

- **Food, by far the biggest line.** A build surplus of quality protein and carbohydrate ([[Part 1.2 - Course Design|partitioning]]) is the single largest monthly cost, and it scales with bodyweight. It dwarfs the gear. Budget it first and honestly; an under-fed course wastes the (cheap) drugs anyway.
- **Gym membership**, fixed and predictable: the cost of the 90% having somewhere to happen.
- **Fast-moving daily supplements**, the things you genuinely use *every day* and re-buy monthly: [[Part 3.6 - Electrolyte Management|electrolytes]] (salt blend, potassium, magnesium), **creatine**, **omega-3**, and the liver and antioxidant supports during oral phases. Individually cheap, collectively a real monthly line.

> [!important] The honest cost hierarchy
> Ranked by what actually leaves your account each month: **food ≫ gym and daily consumables > bloodwork > the pharmaceutical core.**[^10] People brace for the gear to bankrupt them; in a *moderate, longevity-minded* run it is the cheapest recurring part. ==If the food and consumables budget scares you, that is a signal you are not ready to run the gear yet==; feeding and supporting the physique is the prerequisite, not an afterthought ([[Part 1.0 - The Decision|Part 1]]).

## Part 1.3 Takeaways

> [!check] Key concepts to internalize
> 
> - **A course is run on four clocks, not one protocol**: the signal on the eight-week panel, the food on the weekly logbook, the accelerators by phase, the shield every morning. Each has a lever, a gauge and a trigger, and together they run the course without a fixed recipe.
> - **The signal goes lateral, not vertical**: hold a tolerated testosterone base and add a lateral compound rather than escalating testosterone into harsh AIs; adjust it on blood, not feel, one compound at a time, bioidenticals before exotics, the 19-nor near-last.
> - **The food lever comes before the drug lever.** Stalled progress is a food question first; the ladder is food, then training, then dose, and "milk the dose" means squeezing the current one dry before adding to it.
> - **Calculated unhealthiness is the cue to end the course,** not to push harder.
> - **Deployment intensity is set by your body-fat tier**: escalate accelerators to match the phase, never the reverse, and pull them off the moment a marker turns.
> - **The shield runs 365 days and holds the veto**: it is a daily anchor, not a reaction, because the kidneys and vessels do not regenerate and stacked risks multiply.
> - **Blood work runs on a clock that never stops**: baseline, four weeks into anything new, the week-8 decision panel, monthly liver and glucose on orals or GH, a quarterly floor, before any change, and at the end of every cruise.
> - **Cost is an uncertainty problem, not an affordability problem.** A budgeting plan and a stock plan make it predictable; plan the course first, then list every variable, including the "if X, then plus RM Y" changes.
> - **Budget on burn rate, not the receipt:** monthly cost = cart ÷ supply-days × 30. Bulk boxes look pricey upfront and cost little per month; tablet-splitting lowers the dose and stretches supply.
> - **The honest hierarchy: food ≫ gym and consumables > bloodwork > the pharma core.** If the food and consumables number scares you, you are not ready for the gear.

## Your Running-the-Course Task List

1. **Write your four gauges on one card**: hematocrit and E2, logbook and body-fat %, the current deployment tier, BP and lipids. That card *is* your operating panel.
2. **Before any change, name the clock** the problem lives on; most "raise the dose" instincts are really food problems.
3. **Set the food lever as the default first move** when progress stalls and you are still lean, and write the ladder (food, training, dose) where you will see it.
4. **Add one compound at a time, bloods four weeks in**, bioidenticals before exotics.
5. **Match the deployment tier to the body-fat tier**: light, medium, heavy, decided by body fat, detailed in [[Part 2.2 - The Extra Compounds|Part 2.2]].
6. **Anchor the shield to your morning routine** so it is a schedule, never a reaction ([[Part 3.3 - Protection|Part 3.3]], [[Part 3.5 - Choosing Your Ancillaries|Part 3.5]]).
7. **Write the blood-work cadence into the calendar**: baseline, four-week, eight-week, monthly liver and glucose on a course, the quarterly floor, change-points, end of cruise.
8. **List every money variable**, including the mid-course changes, then convert the plan to quantities and compute each item's monthly burn rate.
9. **Stock the shield first**, then the gear, then the monitoring and consumables; mark run-out dates on a calendar and split the splittable tablets.
10. **Budget food first** (the biggest line and the 90%), then gym, then the monthly consumables, then the cheap-per-month pharma; if the food and consumables number is uncomfortable, pause, because that is the readiness signal from [[Part 1.0 - The Decision|Part 1]], not a reason to cut corners on protection.
