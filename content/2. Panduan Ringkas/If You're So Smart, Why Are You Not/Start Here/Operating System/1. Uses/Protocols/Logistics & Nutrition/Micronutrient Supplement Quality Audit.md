# Supplement Quality Audit

Purpose: judge whether the current stock uses acceptable forms, or whether a higher-cost form would meaningfully improve absorption, mechanism, tolerability, standardization, or monitoring reliability.

Rule of thumb: do not upgrade a form just because it sounds premium. Upgrade only when the current form is clearly inferior, the dose is hard to hit, tolerability is poor, or a lab/outcome target justifies it.

## Executive Summary

This note was widened on 2026-06-21 from a micronutrient-only pass into a full supplement quality audit. The earlier version missed too much of the actual stack: Apigenin, Cognitive Stack, Cycle Support, Boswellia, Vitamin E, Beetroot, and the practical half-serving B-complex strategy.

Your micronutrient stack is mostly good, but this audit still has one important correction: the magnesium note is a mixed ledger, not a clean single-SKU quality profile. The current/new magnesium restock is Doctor's Best High Absorption Magnesium Lysinate Glycinate (Albion TRAACS), which is a strong form. The older Nutricost `Magnesium+` lot is now treated as lower-confidence/superseded because the exact purchased SKU may have been an oxide + glycinate blend rather than the cleaner bisglycinate label image captured in the note.

Strong forms that still hold up: zinc picolinate, selenium as L-selenomethionine, vitamin D3 + K2 MK-7, methylfolate/methylcobalamin, and triglyceride-form fish oil.

The main weak points are not "cheap bad forms." They are:

- **Botanical/extract standardization:** Bacopa, Ginkgo, Ashwagandha, Saffron, Coleus, Boswellia, Apigenin, and Beetroot should not be audited like minerals. For these, the key question is whether the label gives the active marker: bacosides, ginkgo flavone glycosides/terpene lactones, withanolides, crocin/safranal, forskolin, boswellic acids/AKBA, apigenin extract identity, and nitrate content.
- **Copper dose/ratio:** NOW Copper Glycinate is a good form, but the exact bottle is 3 mg/tablet. That is a lot relative to the 0.9 mg RDA, so it should stay paired to zinc logic rather than become a casual daily mineral.
- **Magnesium provenance:** new Doctor's Best TRAACS magnesium is good; old Nutricost `Magnesium+` should be considered lower-confidence/superseded unless the physical bottle label confirms it is not an oxide-heavy blend.
- **Fish oil density:** serving size is 2 softgels. Per 2-softgel serving: 2,200 mg fish oil concentrate, 640 mg total omega-3, EPA 360 mg, DHA 240 mg, other omega-3 40 mg. Per softgel: 1,100 mg oil, 320 mg total omega-3, EPA 180 mg, DHA 120 mg. If the target is lipids/inflammation, the upgrade is higher EPA/DHA density, not necessarily a different absorption form.
- **CoQ10:** ubiquinone is acceptable, especially with BioPerine and food, but CoQ10 is formulation-sensitive. Ubiquinol or a better solubilized ubiquinone formula is only worth it if LDL/statin/heart output/mitochondrial-fatigue goals justify the cost.
- **B6/P5P exposure:** active form is good, but the dosing has to be precise. Half serving Life Extension B-complex gives about 50 mg total B6 from a pyridoxine HCl + P5P blend, not 50 mg pure P5P. Adding one separate 50 mg P5P capsule targets about 100 mg/day total B6-active exposure. If the real goal is exactly 100 mg P5P-only, the B-complex contribution cannot be counted as pure P5P.
- **NR:** form is appropriate as an NAD precursor, but this is not a foundational micronutrient. Keep it as optional/longevity tier, not a must-have.
- **Sports nutrition actives:** creatine, beta-alanine, citrulline, taurine, glycine, glutamine, glycerol, MCT, and BHB are mostly form-straightforward. Beetroot is the exception because the performance mechanism depends on nitrate content, and the current powder note does not yet prove nitrate yield per serving.

## Audit Confidence Scale

Use this scale going forward so we do not accidentally give a generic plant extract the same confidence as a mineral chelate.

| Grade | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A | Exact active/form is label-backed and matches the mechanism. | Zinc picolinate, selenium as L-selenomethionine, P5P 50 mg, TUDCA 250 mg, creatine monohydrate. |
| B | Ingredient is correct, but potency, density, or delivery is only moderate. | Fish oil TG form but modest EPA/DHA density; ubiquinone CoQ10 with BioPerine. |
| C | Generic extract/root/powder where the mechanism depends on an unlisted active marker. | Bacopa without bacoside %, Ginkgo without EGb-style standardization, Beetroot powder without nitrate yield. |
| D | Superseded or suspicious form until the physical label proves otherwise. | Old Nutricost Magnesium+ if it is oxide + glycinate. |

## What I Would Actually Upgrade First

1. **Magnesium cleanup / provenance.**  
   The live stock note mixes old Nutricost and new Doctor's Best. The new Doctor's Best TRAACS product is the quality reference. If the old Nutricost bottle physically says oxide + glycinate, flag it as low-quality and do not use it as the model for future magnesium purchases.

2. **Botanical/extract standardization.**  
   The biggest hidden quality gap is not vitamins. It is generic herb/extract labels. Prioritize standardization checks for Boswellia, Bacopa, Ginkgo, Ashwagandha, Saffron, Coleus, Apigenin, and Beetroot.

3. **Fish oil potency, not form.**  
   Current product is good quality but low-to-moderate EPA/DHA density. If you want a stronger cardiovascular/inflammation lever, choose a higher EPA/DHA product and track omega-3 index or at least lipid/inflammation markers.

4. **B6/P5P dose governance.**  
   Half serving B-complex plus one separate P5P capsule is a reasonable way to hit about 100 mg/day total B6 exposure. It is not exact 100 mg P5P-only. Because NIH lists the adult UL for vitamin B6 at 100 mg/day and EFSA is more conservative, keep the high end cycle-specific and monitor symptoms.

5. **CoQ10 only if there is a defined endpoint.**  
   Doctor's Best ubiquinone + BioPerine is reasonable. Upgrade only if you are using CoQ10 as a serious support layer for statin use, fertility, migraine, heart-output concerns, or mitochondrial fatigue. Otherwise the money is better spent elsewhere.

6. **Copper schedule/ratio.**  
   Good form, but the corrected label is 3 mg. Use it because zinc is high, not because copper needs aggressive daily pushing.

## Not Worth Upgrading Right Now

- Vitamin C to liposomal: not necessary unless GI tolerance is bad.
- Methylcobalamin to another B12 form: NIH does not show a clear absorption advantage by B12 form.
- Doctor's Best TRAACS magnesium to a boutique magnesium: unnecessary unless there is a symptom target or GI issue.
- Zinc picolinate to another chelate: unnecessary; solve dose timing, iron competition, and copper balance first.
- Taurine/glycine/TMG/NAC to premium forms: mostly a waste unless changing delivery/cost.

## Evidence Notes

- NIH ODS magnesium: absorption varies by salt; soluble forms tend to absorb better than oxide/sulfate. Source: [NIH ODS Magnesium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS zinc: zinc citrate/gluconate absorption is similar and oxide is lower; high iron can reduce zinc absorption. Source: [NIH ODS Zinc](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS copper: many copper forms exist, but comparative bioavailability studies are lacking; copper absorption falls as intake rises. Source: [NIH ODS Copper](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Copper-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS selenium: common selenium forms include selenomethionine, selenium yeast, selenite, and selenate; absorption can reach about 90%. Source: [NIH ODS Selenium](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Selenium-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS vitamin D: D3 generally raises and maintains 25(OH)D better than D2; fat improves absorption. Source: [NIH ODS Vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS vitamin C: ascorbic acid and mineral ascorbates are standard supplement forms; high doses can cause GI issues and may increase urinary oxalate in susceptible people. Source: [NIH ODS Vitamin C](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS B12: there is no clear evidence that B12 supplement absorption differs by form. Source: [NIH ODS Vitamin B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS vitamin B6: the U.S. adult UL is 100 mg/day, based on neuropathy concern and uncertainty in long-term harm data; EFSA set a much lower adult UL of 12 mg/day in 2023. This is why the B-complex + P5P strategy is treated as cycle-specific rather than casual indefinite dosing. Source: [NIH ODS Vitamin B6](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS vitamin E: vitamin E exists as tocopherols and tocotrienols, but alpha-tocopherol is the form recognized to meet human requirements; supplement labels and dosing should distinguish alpha-tocopherol from mixed tocopherol/tocotrienol complexes. Source: [NIH ODS Vitamin E](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/).
- NIH ODS omega-3: fish oil supplements contribute EPA/DHA intake; product composition varies, so EPA/DHA amount matters more than marketing. Source: [NIH ODS Omega-3](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/).
- NCCIH/Linus Pauling Institute CoQ10: CoQ10 is generally safe but evidence is endpoint-specific, can interact with warfarin/insulin, and oral supplementation raises plasma/lipoprotein CoQ10 though tissue impact is less certain. Sources: [NCCIH CoQ10](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/coenzyme-q10), [Linus Pauling Institute CoQ10](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/dietary-factors/coenzyme-Q10).
- NCCIH ginkgo: evidence for healthy cognitive performance is uncertain, and ginkgo may increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulant drugs. This supports treating generic ginkgo as optional and standardization-dependent. Source: [NCCIH Ginkgo](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginkgo).
- NCCIH ashwagandha: preparations vary, evidence is strongest but still limited for stress/insomnia, and rare liver injury has been linked to supplements. This supports keeping generic non-standardized ashwagandha occasional unless upgraded and monitored. Source: [NCCIH Ashwagandha](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha).

## Bottom Line

The current stack is not built from junk forms. The micronutrient side is mostly solid. The weaker layer is generic extracts where the label does not prove the active marker. Spend upgrade money on:

1. standardized botanical/extract replacements only when the endpoint matters,
2. higher EPA/DHA fish oil if omega-3 is a serious target,
3. endpoint-driven CoQ10 upgrade only when needed,
4. lab-guided mineral balance,
5. cleaning up dose governance for copper, zinc, B-complex, and P5P.
